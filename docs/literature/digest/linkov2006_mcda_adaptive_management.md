# Linkov et al. (2006) — From comparative risk assessment to multi-criteria decision analysis and adaptive management: Recent developments and applications

**Full citation:** Linkov, I., Satterstrom, F.K., Kiker, G., Batchelor, C., Bridges, T., Ferguson, E. (2006). *From comparative risk assessment to multi-criteria decision analysis and adaptive management: Recent developments and applications*. Environment International, 32, pp. 1072–1093. DOI: `10.1016/j.envint.2006.06.013`. Available online 14 Aug 2006. Affiliations: Intertox Inc.; Cambridge Environmental Inc.; University of Florida; U.S. Army Engineer Research and Development Center.

## Problem
Environmental risk managers must combine four very different types of information — modeling/monitoring results, risk analysis, cost/cost-benefit analysis, and stakeholder preferences — to choose among project alternatives. Comparative risk assessment (CRA) builds a decision matrix of alternatives-by-criteria but has no structured method to pick an optimum; the paper asks how multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA), combined with adaptive management, can fill that gap.

## Method
A multidisciplinary literature review plus a proposed decision-analytic framework, illustrated with a case study on contaminated-sediment management in the New York/New Jersey Harbor. It surveys three MCDA technique families: (1) Multi-Attribute Utility Theory (MAUT) — a "compensatory" method that aggregates all criteria onto one utility/value scale, assuming a rational, consistent decision-maker; poor performance on one criterion can be offset by good performance on another; (2) the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) — also compensatory, but built from pairwise comparisons of criteria (e.g., "which is more important: public acceptability or cost?") compiled into a comparison matrix; (3) outranking methods — "partially compensatory," used when criteria cannot be easily aggregated onto a common scale or use incommensurate units. It then reviews (Table 3, PDF p. 7) real applications of these methods to environmental remediation decisions, and couples MCDA with adaptive management (active vs. passive; PDF p. ~9–10), arguing that because ecosystem response to management is uncertain, decisions should be revisited as monitoring data accumulates rather than optimized once and fixed.

## Key results / numbers
No single "result" — it is a methods/synthesis review with worked-example tables (criteria used in the harbor case study: cancer-risk pathways, ecological hazard quotients, cost in $/cubic yard, land-area-per-project-capacity, etc., PDF p. 4). Its normative claim is that MCDA "provides better-supported techniques for the comparison of project alternatives based on decision matrices" and "structured methods for the incorporation of project stakeholders' opinions" (abstract) compared to plain CRA.

## Relevance to our case
Directly supports the case's rule (if MCDA is used) that criteria, normalization method, and the origin of weights must be disclosed and stress-tested — this paper is the methodological reason why: different MCDA families (compensatory MAUT/AHP vs. partially-compensatory outranking) can produce different rankings from the same underlying data, so the choice and its provenance must be explicit. Also supports framing plan revision as adaptive management rather than a single fixed answer.

## Quotable statements
- "CRA lacks a structured method for arriving at an optimal project alternative. Multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA) provides better-supported techniques for the comparison of project alternatives..." (abstract)
- "[T]he inherent uncertainty in our ability to predict ecosystem evolution and response to different management policies requires shifting from optimization-based management to an adaptive management paradigm." (abstract)
- On AHP: "the user uses a numerical scale to compare the choices, and AHP moves systematically through all pair-wise comparisons of criteria and alternatives." (PDF p. 5)

## Cautions
- Domain is environmental remediation (contaminated sediments), not space logistics or engineering economics — used here purely for its decision-methodology content, not its substantive case study.
- Gives no stakeholder weight vector, criteria set, or normalization scheme specific to a fuel-depot supply-planning problem; any weights used in TerraPlan's MCDA (if implemented) must be constructed and justified by the team, not read off this paper.
- MCDA is optional in this case; this paper does not make it mandatory.
- Independently corroborated as a real, correctly cited source: Tanaka (2014) (see B8 / this folder's other papers) cites "Linkov, 2006" in his own reference list for the same CRA-to-MCDA-to-adaptive-management transition, in the context of complex-project risk management.
